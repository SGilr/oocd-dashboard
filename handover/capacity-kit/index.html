Prison demand and the front end of the system

A recurring claim holds that increasing out of court resolution by some proportion reduces prison demand by some other proportion. No published estimate of that relationship exists. This page sets out what is measured, then lets you test for yourself what would have to be true for the claim to hold.

Ink means measured Every figure set in ink comes from a published statistical source, named in the notes with its date and price year.
Rust means modelled Every figure set in rust is produced by the calculator below from parameters you set. None of it is a forecast, and none of it is evidence.
Why the distinction is on the page A health warning in a banner gets separated from the number it qualifies. A warning built into the colour system travels with it.

03  /  The calculator

What would have to be true

This tool refuses to answer the question in the direction it is usually asked. It will not tell you how many places a given increase in diversion would save, because nobody can source the parameters that would make such a figure meaningful. Instead you set a target, and the tool derives the diversion volume that target implies. Then you can judge whether that volume is available.

Read this as a constraint check, not a forecast. Two of the five parameters below have no published value anywhere in England and Wales. They are marked. The tool works because an unsourceable parameter still has a range, and some targets turn out to be unreachable across the whole of it.

On the same assumptions, what the existing volume buys

→ All 226,300 out of court resolutions issued in the year to December 2025 account for 940 places, or 1.1% of the prison population.
→ At £39,454 per place in direct resource terms, 2024-25 prices, that is £37m of resource. It is not cash. Staffing, maintenance and estate costs fall in steps, so a saving of this kind is only banked at the scale of closing an establishment.

How the arithmetic works

Places removed per additional diverted case equal the immediate effect, that is the counterfactual custody share multiplied by time served, plus the downstream effect, that is the reoffending reduction multiplied by the custody rate for averted reoffences multiplied by time served on them. The number of cases needed is the target divided by that per-case figure. Every quantity is stated in place-years per year, so a result of 1,000 means 1,000 fewer occupied places at any given moment, sustained.

04  /  The evidence that does exist

The downstream channel

Two quasi-experimental studies come closest to the question. Both are from the United States, both find substantial benefits from diversion, and neither supports a prison places claim as usually stated. The second finding in each card is the one that tends to be left out.

Felony diversion and the second chance

Harris County, Texas  ·  Mueller-Smith and Schnepel, 2021

A regression discontinuity on first-time felony defendants, using a 1994 penal code reform and a 2007 failed jail expansion ballot as two natural experiments, with a ten year follow-up.

Diversion cut the probability of any future conviction by around 45%, cut total future convictions by around 75%, and raised quarterly employment by around 49%.
On custody it found no significant difference in time served in the initial follow-up, and in the second sample only temporal displacement, with no cumulative difference after four months.

Non-prosecution of minor offences

Suffolk County, Massachusetts  ·  Agan, Doleac and Harvey, 2023

Sixty-seven thousand nonviolent misdemeanour cases, identified through the as-if random assignment of cases to prosecutors of differing leniency, over a two year window.

For the marginal defendant, non-prosecution produced a 29 percentage point fall in the likelihood of a new criminal complaint, a reduction of 53%, and 1.7 fewer complaints.
The outcome measured is subsequent contact with the system, not prison places. The jurisdiction, the offence definitions and the prosecutorial structure are not those of England and Wales.

What the domestic evaluations measured

England and Wales  ·  2018 to 2024

The Checkpoint randomised controlled trial, Operation Turning Point, the two-tier pilot evaluations and the Ministry of Justice reoffending analyses all examine reoffending, process and cost at the point of resolution.

A conditional caution saves around 33 minutes of officer time against a charge, roughly 6%. The saving comes from resolving without arrest, not from the conditions attached.
None of them measured prison demand. The Ministry of Justice appraised out of court reform once under Green Book rules and returned a ten year net present value of minus £109.19 million in 2019/20 prices.
05  /  Method and limits

What this page is not

Three parameters the public evidence base does not contain

  • The unit cost of a caution, a community resolution or a magistrates' court case. The most recent published court unit costs are National Audit Office figures in 2015/16 prices, and the Ministry of Justice states in both 2026 Courts and Tribunals Bill impact assessments that per-case costs for HMCTS, the Crown Prosecution Service and legal aid are not disclosed.
  • The counterfactual disposal for cases resolved out of court. Forces do not systematically record cases assessed and refused, with coded reasons, which is the module that would let anyone estimate selection.
  • A causal estimate of the reoffending difference between out of court resolution and prosecution for comparable cases in England and Wales.

A closing note on money. A prison place cost £39,454 in direct resource terms and £59,026 overall in 2024-25 prices. Neither figure is cash-realisable at the margin: staffing, maintenance and estate costs fall in steps, and the saving is only banked at the scale of closing an establishment. A page of this kind could easily carry a ticking savings counter. It would be the weakest thing on it, so there is not one.
